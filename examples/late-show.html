Showing the rewards of user-centred service design at scale

Services in government · 5 March 2021

Ben Stevens · Aspasia Dellaporta · Nadia Huq

Scene 1 · A legacy system · December 2017

A Home Office team regulates scientific research.

Its licensing system is a legacy one. In December 2017, work begins to replace it.

Universities and commercial organisations apply for three types of licence: establishment, personal and project.

Scene 2 · The discovery· 2017

A discovery into the legacy service.

The Home Office completes it in 2017. It names the problems.

Staff had to manually copy lengthy Word applications into other formats to issue licences.

No single source of truth
Users could be looking at outdated versions of licences.
Inflexible system
Applications cannot be shared securely in-system, so sensitive data risks being shared via other channels.
Restrictive account management
A third-party supplier manages the accounts.
Insufficient guidance
Users are unclear on what to include, so they submit more revisions. It is time-consuming.
Service Standard non-compliant
The legacy service meets only 3 out of 18 points.

Scene 3 · The research· 3 years

Users at the heart of the transformation.

The team holds more than 500 research sessions in 3 years.

It reaches 61% of establishments across the UK.

More than 80 sessions are remote, to widen the reach and accommodate lockdown restrictions.

Service map: reimagining the new licensing service. Image from the original post, Open Government Licence v3.0. The full map, with needs and activities, is there.

“This is a great example of the value of a user-centred approach. In close collaboration with the service owner, Martin Whiting, this team have worked extremely hard, conducting user research with more than 500 participants and building trust in our ability to create digital services that are genuinely transformative.”

Scene 4 · Interviews and workshops

Opposing views about who should be able to see licence data.

Some establishments need to protect intellectual property.

Others need to share data to support learning and best practice.

We didn’t shy away from this complexity. Instead we kept testing solutions with users until we found something that met everyone’s needs.

The team, in the post

Scene 5 · Public Beta· 2018 to 2020

On time, on budget, all 14 points.

The new service meets all 14 points of the updated Service Standard. It is in Public Beta.

Scene 6 · Lessons learned

What the team takes from it.

Benefits take time to realise
At first, applications take longer to process in the new service. Ways of working change, and that takes time to embed.
We all need to compromise
At full strength the team is 11 people. Every discipline has to compromise.
Preferences are not needs
The team limits the features it adds, to avoid making the service too complex.
Senior buy-in is vital
Senior stakeholders back an evidence-based, user-centred approach. The team can make decisions that benefit all users, not just the most influential or vocal ones.

Next, the team keeps developing the service, and speeds up knowledge transfer so the regulator can manage it with less support.
